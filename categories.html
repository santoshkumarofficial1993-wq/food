Ten American cuisines, one flag

"American food" is a useless category. A Lowcountry shrimp boil and a Minnesota hotdish share a country and almost nothing else. Here is how we file every recipe, and what actually defines each region beyond the clichés.

How we decide where a dish belongs

Regional attribution is where most recipe sites get lazy. We use three tests, and a dish has to pass at least two before we label it.

  1. Documented origin. A printed source — a community cookbook, a newspaper column, a restaurant menu — placing the dish in that region before 1980.
  2. Ingredient logic. The dish depends on something the region actually had. Lowcountry cooking uses shrimp and stone-ground grits because both were local staples, not because they sound Southern.
  3. Living practice. People there still cook it at home, in roughly this form, today.

When a dish fails all three but is popularly associated with a region anyway, we say so on the recipe page instead of quietly perpetuating it. There are eleven of those and they are some of our most-read pages.

We are missing a region and we know it

Alaskan, Puerto Rican and Great Plains Indigenous cooking are all under-represented here, because we will not publish a regional recipe we have not researched properly. If you cook in those traditions and want to work with us, we would like to hear from you — this is paid work, not a content submission form.
