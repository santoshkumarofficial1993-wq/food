A test kitchen in a rented house

Skillet & Stars is six people, one converted garage in Asheville, North Carolina, and a domestic range that is deliberately ordinary. We started in 2016 because American home cooking deserved better documentation than it was getting.

Why we exist

In 2016, Dana was trying to reproduce her grandmother's chicken and dumplings from a card that said "flour — enough" and "bake until done". She spent four weekends on it. The versions she found online were worse than the card: 1,200 words of childhood memory, an ingredient list scaled for a restaurant, and a method that had clearly never been followed by anyone who was not standing next to the person who wrote it.

The problem is structural. Recipe publishing rewards volume and search visibility, and testing is the most expensive thing you can do. So most sites do not test — they adapt, aggregate, and photograph. The recipe works for the person who wrote it, in their kitchen, and then it goes out into the world untested.

We decided to do the opposite thing and see whether enough people cared. Roughly 1.9 million a month, it turns out, do.

What "tested twice" means precisely

It is a specific claim, so it needs a specific definition. A recipe is published only after:

  1. A development cook, where the dish is built and the draft written. This usually takes between three and eleven attempts.
  2. A blind verification cook by a second person who was not present for development, working only from the written draft, with no verbal help of any kind. If they hesitate, ask a question, or produce a different result, the draft is wrong and gets rewritten.
  3. A final read-through against our style rules: weights before volumes, real elapsed times including preheating, and every visual cue described (what it looks like, not only how long it takes).

About one recipe in nine fails verification and goes back for another round. Eleven recipes have been abandoned entirely because we could not make them work reliably in a home kitchen, and we have written up why.

If the second cook has to ask a question, the recipe is not finished. That rule has cost us more publishing days than everything else combined.

How we are funded — the whole picture

A recipe site's business model shapes its recipes, so ours should be visible. Here is 2025, rounded to the nearest percentage point.

Revenue sources for 2025
SourceShare of revenueWhat it means for you
Display advertising (Google AdSense & Ad Manager)44%Three ad units maximum per page, all labelled. Removed for members.
Kitchen Pass memberships34%Never gates a recipe. Buys convenience features.
Cookbook sales11%Sold directly by us.
Affiliate commissions7%Disclosed above every product list, never inside recipes.
Sponsored recipe development4%Four per year maximum, labelled at the top of the page.

Two things follow from that mix, and we would rather state them than have you infer them. First, advertising pays for a large share of the site, which is why free readers see ads — and why we have written rules about where those ads can and cannot appear (never inside a method, never between an ingredient list and its steps, never styled to look like our own content). Second, because no single advertiser is more than about 2% of revenue, we can afford to say no, and we do — roughly forty times a month.

What we will not do

  • Publish untested recipes. Including for holidays, including under deadline pressure.
  • Generate recipes with AI. Every recipe on this site was developed by a person who cooked it. We use software for spellcheck and nutrition estimation, and that is the extent of it.
  • Make health claims. We publish estimated nutrition figures because readers ask for them. We do not sell diet plans, and we are not qualified to give dietary advice.
  • Take payment for a rating. Equipment tests are not for sale at any price.
  • Put recipes behind the paywall. Ever. It is in the editorial policy in writing.

The people who cook it

Everyone here cooks. Nobody here only writes about cooking.

Dana Whitfield Founder, head of testing

Twelve years on restaurant lines in Charleston and Nashville before this. Develops most of the Southern and barbecue archive. Owns the smash burger obsession.

Ray Okonkwo Test cook, verification lead

Runs the blind verification cooks, which makes him the person most likely to send a draft back. Former pastry cook. Responsible for the baking archive.

Marguerite Bell Baking and pastry

Taught baking science at a community college for nine years. Wrote our pie pastry method and the altitude adjustment notes that go with it.

Nora Vasquez Regional research

Food historian. Does the archival work that decides whether we can call a dish Cajun, Creole or neither. Reads community cookbooks for pleasure.

Tom Reilly Photography

Shoots everything in the kitchen it was cooked in, under available light. Also photographs the failures, which is a surprisingly large part of the job.

Aisha Grant Editor

Cuts the childhood stories, keeps the technique notes, and enforces the rule that a method step never contains two instructions.

A short timeline

Ten years, compressed.

Company timeline
2016Dana publishes 30 recipes from a rented kitchen in West Asheville. No advertising, no plan.
2017Ray joins. The blind verification cook becomes standard practice — and the publishing rate immediately halves.
2019First display advertising. The rules about where ads may appear are written the same week and have not changed since.
2020Traffic quadruples. We publish the pantry series for people cooking from whatever was left in the cupboard.
2021Kitchen Pass launches with the commitment that no recipe ever goes behind it.
2022Nora joins; the regional attribution standard is written and 140 existing recipes are re-labelled.
2023First printed cookbook. It sells out in nine days and we badly underestimate shipping.
2024Equipment testing programme formalised. We start buying every test unit at retail.
20251,412 recipes. The correction log is made public, which we should have done in 2016.

Work with us

We hire test cooks and regional contributors two or three times a year, and we pay for recipe development rather than asking for submissions. If you cook in a tradition we are missing — Alaskan, Puerto Rican, Great Plains Indigenous — write to us regardless of whether a role is posted.
