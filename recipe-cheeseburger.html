The 6-Minute Smash Burger

The crust is the whole point. You cannot get it on a grill, you cannot get it from a thick patty, and you cannot get it if the pan is merely hot. This is the version we landed on after nineteen cooks and one ruined smoke alarm.

By Dana Whitfield Published 14 July 2025 Tested 3 times 4.9 ★ (1,208 ratings)

Prep8 min
Cook6 min
Total14 min
Serves2
Jump to method Read the notes first

Why this works

A smash burger is a Maillard delivery system. Thin patty, maximum surface contact, and a pan hot enough that the beef's moisture flashes off instead of steaming the meat. Three variables decide whether you get lace-edged crust or grey meat:

  • Fat ratio. 80/20 chuck. 90/10 dries out at this thickness before a crust forms; anything fattier than 75/25 floods the pan and the patty poaches.
  • Pan material. Carbon steel and cast iron hold heat through the smash. Thin stainless drops 90°F the moment the meat lands and never recovers. We measured this with a surface thermometer across five pans — the results are in the equipment note below.
  • Timing of the smash. Within five seconds of the ball touching the pan. After that the exterior has already set and you are pressing juice out instead of spreading raw meat.

Salt goes on after the smash, never mixed into the ground beef. Salt dissolves myosin, the meat binds like sausage, and you get a springy patty instead of a crumbly one. This is the single most common failure in reader emails about this recipe.

Nineteen cooks. The only variable that mattered more than pan temperature was how long the ball sat in the pan before it got flattened. Test kitchen log, 9–14 July 2025

Ingredients

Tap any line to cross it off while you shop or cook.

For the patties

  • 454 g ground chuck, 80/20 (1 lb) — from the butcher counter if possible, ground that morning
  • 2 tsp neutral oil with a high smoke point — grapeseed, refined peanut or canola
  • Kosher salt — about 1.5 tsp total, applied after smashing
  • Coarse black pepper, 16-mesh if you have it

To build

  • 2 potato buns (Martin's if you can find them; brioche is too sweet and collapses)
  • 4 slices American cheese — the processed kind, deliberately. It emulsifies; cheddar breaks and goes oily.
  • 1 small white onion, sliced paper thin on a mandoline
  • 8 dill pickle chips, patted dry
  • Yellow mustard and softened butter for the buns
Substitutions we have actually tested Ground brisket at 80/20 works and tastes deeper, but costs roughly twice as much. Plant-based grounds smash acceptably if you chill them hard first and add 1 tsp oil to the pan; skip the salt-after rule because they contain salt already. For dairy-free, a slice of cashew-based cheese melts adequately at this pan temperature — the four other brands we tried did not melt at all.

Method

  1. Divide and chill the beef

    Break the chuck into four loose 113 g balls. Do not roll, compress or knead them — a smash burger ball should look like it fell together by accident. Set them on a plate and refrigerate, uncovered, for 20 minutes. Cold, dry meat crusts faster.

  2. Toast the buns

    Butter the cut faces. Toast in a dry pan over medium heat until deep gold, about 90 seconds. Do this first, while the pan is still clean, and set them aside cut-side up. A toasted bun is a moisture barrier; an untoasted one turns to paste under two patties.

  3. Get the pan properly hot

    Heat carbon steel or cast iron over high heat for a full four minutes. Test it: a drop of water should skate across the surface as a bead rather than sizzling in place. Add the oil and swirl. It should shimmer and just begin to smoke. Open a window now, not later.

  4. Smash — within five seconds

    Place two balls in the pan with space between them. Immediately press each one flat with a stiff metal spatula backed by a second spatula or a heavy pan, to about 6 mm thick and wider than your bun. Hold the pressure for ten seconds. Salt and pepper the exposed side heavily.

  5. Flip once, cheese immediately

    After 90 seconds the edges will be lace-brown and the top will have turned from red to pink. Slide the spatula under at a low angle and scrape — the crust is stuck to the pan on purpose and you want all of it. Flip, lay a slice of cheese on each patty, and cook 30 seconds more.

  6. Repeat, then build

    Wipe the pan with a paper towel held in tongs, re-oil, and cook the second pair. Stack two patties per bun. Pickles and mustard on the bottom bun, onion on top of the cheese so the residual heat softens it slightly.

  7. Rest 60 seconds

    Press the top bun down gently and leave it alone for a minute. The cheese sets just enough to hold the stack together, and the bun absorbs exactly the right amount of fat. Cut it in half only if you want to see the cross-section; it eats better whole.

Cook's notes

Getting the crust without a smoke alarm incident

This recipe produces smoke. That is not a flaw, it is rendered beef fat hitting a 450°F surface. Run the extractor on high before you start, and if you have a portable induction burner, cook outside on the porch — a genuinely common approach among the people who emailed us about this recipe.

Doing four burgers at once

Do not crowd the pan. Two patties in a 12-inch skillet is the maximum; four drops the surface temperature by around 120°F and you will get grey meat. Cook in batches and hold finished burgers in a 200°F oven on a rack — they survive eight minutes without noticeable loss.

Make-ahead and storage

Formed balls keep, uncovered, in the fridge for up to 24 hours and actually crust better on day two because the surface dries out. Cooked patties do not reheat well — they go from crusted to leathery in about 40 seconds under any heat source. If you have leftovers, chop them cold into a hash with potato the next morning.
