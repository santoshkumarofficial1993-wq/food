Equipment test

We seasoned nine cast iron skillets eleven ways

Fourteen months, 4,100 cooking cycles, nine identical pans and a bench abrasion rig built out of a drill press. Flaxseed oil — the internet's standard answer for a decade — finished ninth.

By Dana Whitfield 18 June 2025 11 min read

What is in this piece

  1. Why we redid a test everyone has already done
  2. The method, including what we got wrong first time
  3. Results, ranked
  4. What happened to flaxseed
  5. What this means in a normal kitchen

Why we redid a test everyone has already done

The cast iron seasoning question has been answered publicly at least a dozen times, and almost all of those answers rest on the same 2010 blog post recommending flaxseed oil on the basis of its high iodine value — a measure of how readily an oil polymerises. The chemistry is sound. The conclusion does not survive contact with a kitchen.

What nobody had published, as far as we could find, was a durability test: not "which oil forms a slick coating fastest" but "which coating is still there after a year of scrubbing, acidic sauces and metal spatulas". Those are different questions and they have different answers.

So we bought nine identical 10-inch pans, stripped them all to bare iron in a lye bath, and started over.

Almost every published recommendation measures how quickly a seasoning forms. Almost nobody measures whether it survives.

The method, including what we got wrong first time

Each pan received one of eleven treatments — nine oils plus two control conditions. Every pan got six seasoning rounds at 450°F, one hour each, wiped to a level most people would call "too thin", because too much oil is the single most common seasoning mistake and it produces a coating that flakes.

Then the pans entered rotation in the test kitchen for fourteen months. Each pan cooked the same weekly schedule:

  • Two high-heat sears (steak or smash burgers, roughly 450°F surface)
  • One acidic cook — tomato sauce simmered 25 minutes, which is the condition most likely to strip seasoning
  • One egg test, scored for release on a five-point scale by whoever cooked it
  • Hot water and a chainmail scrubber after every use; soap once a week

What we got wrong first time: our original protocol had each tester use whichever pan was nearest. Four months in, we realised pan 3 had been used roughly twice as often as pan 7, which invalidated the comparison. We restarted with a rota pinned to the wall. That mistake cost us four months and is the reason this piece is a year later than planned.

The headline: the top three are all cheap, widely available, refined neutral oils, and the differences between them are small enough that you should buy whichever is nearest. The pan that came pre-seasoned from the factory and was simply used, without any home seasoning at all, beat five of the nine deliberate treatments.

What happened to flaxseed

For seven months, the flaxseed pan looked the best in the kitchen. Glassy, near-black, genuinely beautiful. In month seven it began to flake — not wear, flake, in sheets the size of a fingernail, taking bare iron patches with them.

The explanation is the same property that made it attractive. Flaxseed oil polymerises into a hard, highly cross-linked film. Hard films are brittle. Under repeated thermal cycling, a brittle film accumulates micro-cracks and eventually delaminates from the substrate, all at once. The softer, less complete polymerisation from grapeseed or canola flexes instead of cracking.

This is not a new idea — it is standard coatings behaviour, and it is why nobody paints a bridge with the hardest available material. It simply had not been tested on skillets over a long enough period for anyone to notice.

An honest limitation Nine pans is a small sample and we ran one pan per treatment, so we cannot separate treatment effects from pan-to-pan variation with any statistical confidence. What we can say is that the flaxseed failure mode was dramatic, sudden and consistent with known coatings physics — and that two readers who repeated the test independently reported the same flaking, at month six and month nine.

What this means in a normal kitchen

Almost nothing, and that is the useful finding. The practical advice from fourteen months of work is four lines long:

  1. Use whatever cheap refined neutral oil you already own. Grapeseed, canola and sunflower are indistinguishable in practice.
  2. Wipe it thinner than feels right. If it looks wet, it is too much, and too much is what causes flaking far more often than the wrong oil does.
  3. Cook in the pan. Cooking fatty things does more for a seasoning than any oven ritual. The best-performing pan in our kitchen is the one that gets used daily.
  4. Stop worrying about soap. Modern dish soap does not strip polymerised seasoning. We washed with soap weekly for fourteen months and it changed nothing measurable.

If you already have a pan you like, do not strip it because of this article. The single strongest predictor of a good cast iron surface in our test was not the oil — it was how often the pan got cooked in.

What we are testing next

Carbon steel, using the same protocol, plus a follow-up on whether the grapeseed result holds on an induction hob, where the heat distribution is meaningfully different.
